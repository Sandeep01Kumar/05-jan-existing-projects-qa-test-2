## Description

This is the pipeline's CROSS-CUTTING reliability epic — it observes the health of all preceding pipeline stages (`EPIC-PA-01` capture through `EPIC-PA-08` alerting) and codifies the team's service-level objectives (SLOs), error-budget policy, and observability practices. Two flagship SLOs anchor the epic: a **capture-rate SLO** that quantifies the percentage of actual crashes that are captured by the SDK and successfully ingested end-to-end, and a **time-to-dashboard SLO** that adopts the canonical 5-minute end-to-end latency target documented in the Firebase Crashlytics troubleshooting guidance (the same 5-minute window that anchors `EPIC-PA-07` Dashboard Delivery and `EPIC-MIG-09` Test-Crash Validation). Pipeline observability is realized through a combination of SDK debug logging (the Crashlytics debug log channel enabled via `adb shell setprop log.tag.FirebaseCrashlytics DEBUG` on Android or the `-FIRDebugEnabled` launch argument on iOS), Firebase Console queries, BigQuery analyses sourced from the data substrate in `EPIC-PA-06`, and (optionally) downstream Looker Studio or Grafana dashboards that surface real-time health signals to engineering and on-call audiences. Mean-time-to-detect (MTTD) for emerging issues quantifies how quickly the alert system from `EPIC-PA-08` surfaces a new high-impact crash after its first occurrence — a key signal for the alerting configuration's effectiveness and the primary input that lets the team iterate on alert thresholds based on data rather than intuition. Error-budget policy converts SLO breaches into engineering decisions: when budget is consumed beyond a documented threshold, the team prioritizes reliability work over feature work; when budget is healthy, the team focuses on features without guilt. The new Firebase Crashlytics SDK is estimated to capture about 30% more Android crashes than the legacy Fabric SDK, providing a quantified expected improvement target that the post-migration capture-rate SLO baseline must reflect and that `EPIC-MIG-09` test-crash validation feeds into.

## Business Value

Without explicit SLOs, the crash-reporting pipeline's quality is ambient and unmeasurable — engineering decisions become reactive to user complaints and anecdotal Slack messages rather than data-driven, and the team has no defensible answer to "is the pipeline healthy right now?" beyond subjective intuition. The capture-rate SLO ensures the team detects regressions in the SDK integration (for example, a release that accidentally breaks the Crashlytics initialization path on a subset of devices, or a build that ships without the `firebase-crashlytics-gradle` plugin applied) within a single release cycle rather than after weeks of silently missing crashes, which protects the business-critical assumption that the Firebase Console reflects reality. The time-to-dashboard SLO ensures that on-call engineers can trust that a quiet dashboard reflects a quiet production population rather than a stalled ingestion pipeline, which is the difference between a healthy paging signal and an alert-fatigue or silent-failure trap. Error-budget policy creates a principled, fair tradeoff between reliability work and feature work — preventing both excess reliability investment (over-engineering for stability the business does not need) and excess feature pressure (shipping into a degrading reliability picture); it gives engineering leadership a defensible, data-backed conversation with product about when to slow down. MTTD measurement closes the loop on the alerting system in `EPIC-PA-08` — without MTTD tracking, alert thresholds are tuned by intuition (or by complaint volume) rather than by observed outcomes, and the team cannot tell whether an alert configuration is sensitive enough to catch issues early or too noisy to be actionable. Roles that benefit include the release manager (early warning of regression within a release cycle via the capture-rate SLO), the on-call engineer (trustable dashboard freshness via the time-to-dashboard SLO), the reliability engineer (data-driven alert tuning via MTTD), the engineering leader (principled feature/reliability tradeoffs via error-budget policy), and the data analyst (clear, defensible baselines for cross-platform stability comparison).

## In Scope

The following items are included in this epic:

- Capture-rate SLO: a documented target percentage of actual crashes that are captured by the SDK and successfully ingested into the Firebase Crashlytics pipeline end-to-end, measured against an external reality signal (for example, ANR reports from `getHistoricalProcessExitReasons` on Android 11+, OS-level crash logs surfaced by Google Play vitals, or device-level event counts from `EPIC-PA-06` BigQuery export joined against the production event stream). The specific target percentage (e.g., 95%, 99%) is team-specific and is documented as part of this epic's outputs.
- Time-to-dashboard SLO: a documented end-to-end latency target of 5 minutes from the cold relaunch of a crashed app to the appearance of the resulting issue card in the Firebase Console under `DevOps & Engagement → Crashlytics`, adopting the canonical 5-minute window from the Firebase Crashlytics troubleshooting guidance and the same window referenced by `EPIC-PA-07` Dashboard Delivery and `EPIC-MIG-09` Test-Crash Validation.
- Throughput observability: documented queries (against `EPIC-PA-06`'s BigQuery export and against the Firebase Console's own filter UI) that surface uploads-per-second from the SDK fleet, ingest acks-per-second at the server boundary, dashboard refresh latency observed by humans, and any backlog signals that indicate ingestion is keeping up (or not keeping up) with capture.
- Mean-time-to-detect (MTTD) tracking: a documented metric that computes the median elapsed time from the first occurrence of a new (or regressed) issue created by `EPIC-PA-05` grouping to the first alert firing in `EPIC-PA-08`, over a documented look-back window (for example, 30 days), with the trend reviewable in the pipeline health dashboard.
- Error-budget policy: a documented response plan for what the team does when SLOs are breached — including the budget window (for example, 30 days), the budget amount (for example, the team accepts a 1% breach allowance per window), the budget-burn thresholds that trigger named responses (for example, halt feature work above 50% budget burn in the first half of the window), and the named individuals or roles authorized to invoke each response.
- Pipeline health dashboard: an aggregated, single-page view (built in Looker Studio, Grafana, or a comparable visualization tool fed by `EPIC-PA-06` BigQuery data, or assembled from Firebase Console widgets) that displays the capture-rate SLO and current rate, the time-to-dashboard SLO and current latency, MTTD trend, and current error-budget burn — so a single glance answers "is the pipeline healthy right now?".
- Synthetic monitoring: a documented job (running at a documented cadence — for example, periodically against a staging build) that programmatically force-crashes the app via the platform-idiomatic API and records the end-to-end latency from crash to Firebase Console visibility; this is the primary input that drives the time-to-dashboard SLO's continuous validation.
- SLO breach response automation: the alert paths (via `EPIC-PA-08` channels — Slack, Jira, email) that fire when a synthetic monitoring run exceeds the time-to-dashboard SLO, when the capture-rate SLO drops below the documented threshold over the 7-day rolling window, or when the error-budget burn crosses a named threshold.

## Out of Scope

The following items are explicitly excluded from this epic and are typically owned by a sibling or successor epic:

- SDK capture mechanics (the runtime hooks for unhandled exceptions, NDK signal handlers, ANR detection, and non-fatal `recordError`) — owned by `EPIC-PA-01`. This epic OBSERVES the capture-rate that those mechanics produce but does not own the mechanics themselves; capture defects discovered via the capture-rate SLO are referred back to `EPIC-PA-01`.
- Transport mechanics (background upload on next launch, post-app-close upload, exponential backoff retries, HTTPS transport security) — owned by `EPIC-PA-02`. Transport-related contributions to the time-to-dashboard latency budget are referred back to `EPIC-PA-02` when isolated.
- Server-side ingestion (logical ingest endpoints, schema validation, request authentication, deduplication) — owned by `EPIC-PA-04`.
- Issue grouping by stack-trace fingerprint and the issue lifecycle (`Open`, `Closed`, `Regressed`) — owned by `EPIC-PA-05`. This epic CONSUMES the issue events `EPIC-PA-05` emits in order to compute MTTD but does not own the grouping or lifecycle logic.
- Durable persistence, BigQuery export linking, and downstream Looker Studio / Grafana visualization integrations — owned by `EPIC-PA-06`. This epic USES the data substrate that `EPIC-PA-06` produces to compute its SLO and MTTD metrics, but does not own the substrate.
- Firebase Console dashboard surface and Android Studio App Quality Insights surface — owned by `EPIC-PA-07`. This epic's pipeline health dashboard is a separate, supplementary surface that observes the catalog's pipeline end-to-end; the Firebase Console itself remains `EPIC-PA-07`'s responsibility.
- Alert routing, severity-aware channel selection, quiet-hours policy, and velocity-alert configuration (Crashlytics Android SDK v18.6.0+ floor) — owned by `EPIC-PA-08`. This epic OBSERVES alert latency (as the second half of the MTTD computation) but does not own the alert engine or its routing configuration.
- Opt-in collection mode, PII redaction policy, GDPR/CCPA data-subject rights, and the `setCrashlyticsCollectionEnabled` runtime override — owned by `EPIC-PA-09`. This epic respects privacy controls (synthetic monitoring runs only against staging builds with explicit consent or against test accounts) but does not extend or modify the privacy gate.
- The one-time Fabric → Firebase Crashlytics migration itself (Maven repository cleanup, Gradle plugin swap, Xcode Build Phases cleanup, Answers translation) — owned by the migration theme epics `EPIC-MIG-01` through `EPIC-MIG-10`. This epic's post-migration capture-rate baseline depends on `EPIC-MIG-09` test-crash validation but does not perform any migration step itself.

## User Stories

This epic contains 4 user stories. Each story is derived from `../templates/story-template.md` and embedded inline below using H3 headings (Rule AR-1). Stories live INSIDE their parent epic file to keep each epic self-contained and eliminate broken cross-file links during refactors. The four stories partition the epic into its two flagship SLOs (capture-rate in `STORY-PA-10-S01`, time-to-dashboard in `STORY-PA-10-S02`), the alerting-loop closure metric (MTTD in `STORY-PA-10-S03`), and the policy framework that converts SLO breaches into engineering decisions (error-budget policy in `STORY-PA-10-S04`). ### STORY-PA-10-S01: Establish capture-rate SLO and observability

**As a** release manager, **I want** a documented capture-rate SLO with observability into the current capture rate, **so that** regressions in the SDK integration (for example, a release that accidentally breaks the Crashlytics initialization path on a subset of devices) are detected within a single release cycle rather than after weeks of silently missing crashes.

#### Acceptance Criteria

- **Given** a documented capture-rate SLO target (for example, 95% of crashes captured and successfully ingested end-to-end), **When** the release manager queries the pipeline health dashboard, **Then** the dashboard displays the current capture rate aggregated over the last 7 days alongside the SLO target and a visible breach indicator if the current rate is below the target.
- **Given** a new release that accidentally breaks the SDK integration on a subset of devices (for example, a Gradle change that omits the `firebase-crashlytics-gradle` plugin from a build variant, or a release that fails to call `FirebaseApp.configure()` at startup on iOS), **When** the next 7-day rolling window completes after the affected release rolls out, **Then** the dashboard surfaces the capture-rate drop, an alert fires via `EPIC-PA-08` channels to the release-management group, and the alert references the affected release and device population so the on-call engineer can act without manual triage.
- **Given** the canonical Firebase guidance that the new Firebase Crashlytics SDK is estimated to capture about 30% more Android crashes than the legacy Fabric SDK, **When** post-migration capture rates from `EPIC-MIG-09` test-crash validation are compared to the pre-migration Fabric baseline for the same device population and same time-of-day window, **Then** the team can validate the +30% Android capture improvement quantitatively and record the comparison as a one-time baseline reset in the SLO documentation.
- **Given** the capture-rate SLO and its current rate are documented in the pipeline health dashboard, **When** the team conducts its periodic SLO review, **Then** the review minutes record the current rate, the target, the breach status, and any planned response (continue, tune the target if the team agrees the target is calibrated incorrectly, or open a reliability ticket if the rate is genuinely degrading).

#### Notes

- The +30% Android capture improvement (per the Firebase Blog announcement of the new SDK) is a quantified expected outcome of the Fabric → Firebase migration; this story makes that improvement OBSERVABLE in the team's own dashboard rather than only in the public Firebase announcement. The story does NOT prescribe the +30% as the SLO target — the SLO target is a steady-state quality bar, while +30% is a one-time migration delta that is recorded as a baseline reset and then absorbed into steady-state operation.
- The exact capture-rate target (95%, 99%, 99.5%) is team-specific and depends on the app's user-facing reliability claims, the user population's device mix (older Android devices and devices in regions with intermittent connectivity have more capture drag than newer devices on stable networks), and the team's tolerance for false-positive alerts on noisy windows. The story does NOT prescribe a specific value but does require that ONE is documented and that the dashboard displays both the target and the current rate so the SLO is operationally meaningful rather than aspirational.
- Capture-rate measurement requires an external reality signal because Crashlytics cannot measure crashes it failed to capture; the canonical reality signals are Google Play vitals on Android (which surfaces device-level crash counts independent of the SDK), `getHistoricalProcessExitReasons` on Android 11+ (which records process death reasons the OS observed), and OS-level crash report aggregates on iOS where available. The specific reality-signal source is team-specific and is recorded in the SLO documentation.

### STORY-PA-10-S02: Establish time-to-dashboard SLO and synthetic monitoring

**As a** member of the on-call team, **I want** a documented time-to-dashboard SLO with continuous synthetic monitoring, **so that** I can trust that a healthy-looking Firebase Console dashboard reflects current reality rather than a stalled or partial ingestion pipeline that is silently lying to the paging system.

#### Acceptance Criteria

- **Given** the canonical 5-minute end-to-end latency target from the Firebase Crashlytics troubleshooting guidance (the same target referenced by `EPIC-PA-07` Story 6 Dashboard Delivery and by `EPIC-MIG-09` Test-Crash Validation), **When** the team documents the time-to-dashboard SLO, **Then** the SLO captures the 5-minute target as the upper bound on the elapsed time from cold relaunch of a crashed app to the appearance of the resulting issue card in the Firebase Console.
- **Given** a synthetic monitoring job that periodically force-crashes a staging build via the platform-idiomatic API (`throw RuntimeException("Force Crash")` on Android, `fatalError("Force Crash")` on iOS, `FirebaseCrashlytics.instance.crash()` on Flutter, the Firebase Unity test-crash API on Unity, and `crashlytics().crash()` on React Native), **When** the job runs, **Then** the end-to-end latency from cold relaunch to Firebase Console visibility is measured and recorded as a time-series in the pipeline health dashboard alongside the 5-minute SLO line.
- **Given** a synthetic monitoring run whose measured latency exceeds the 5-minute SLO, **When** the breach is detected, **Then** an alert fires via `EPIC-PA-08` channels to the pipeline operations group, the alert includes the affected platform and the observed latency, and the Crashlytics debug logging procedure (`adb shell setprop log.tag.FirebaseCrashlytics DEBUG` on Android, `-FIRDebugEnabled` on iOS) is linked from the alert so the on-call engineer has an immediate reproducible diagnostic path.
- **Given** the time-to-dashboard SLO and its current observed latency are documented in the pipeline health dashboard, **When** the team conducts its periodic SLO review, **Then** the review minutes record the percentile latency (p50, p95, p99) over the review window, the breach count, and the time-of-day or platform pattern of any clustered breaches so trends are visible alongside raw breach counts.

#### Notes

- Per the canonical Firebase troubleshooting guidance, if after five minutes a test crash is still not visible in the Firebase Console the recommended diagnostic is to enable Crashlytics debug logging and re-run the test to observe whether the SDK is serializing the crash on the original crash and attempting upload on cold relaunch; this is the canonical 5-minute target this SLO adopts. The 5-minute window is the documented Firebase visibility-window guarantee, so adopting it as the SLO aligns the team's internal contract with the externally documented behavior.
- Synthetic monitoring complements production observation rather than replacing it. Production observation reveals real-user latency distributions across the actual device and network population; synthetic monitoring isolates the pipeline's own latency from device and network variance by running from a controlled environment. Both signals are needed for a confident time-to-dashboard SLO — the production signal answers "what do real users experience?" and the synthetic signal answers "is the pipeline itself functioning?". A breach in the synthetic signal with the production signal healthy points to environment-specific issues; a breach in both points to a pipeline-side regression.
- Synthetic monitoring must respect the privacy controls in `EPIC-PA-09`: synthetic crashes are produced by test accounts or staging builds where collection is explicitly enabled, not from end-user production builds. The synthetic job's existence and configuration are documented in the pipeline operations runbook so an on-call engineer can confirm a Firebase Console issue card with a stack frame matching the synthetic force-crash signature is the synthetic job, not a real user crash to be investigated.

### STORY-PA-10-S03: Track mean-time-to-detect (MTTD) for emerging issues

**As a** reliability engineer, **I want** to track mean-time-to-detect (MTTD) for emerging crash issues, **so that** I can iterate on alert thresholds and routing in `EPIC-PA-08` based on real observed outcomes rather than tuning thresholds by intuition or by complaint volume.

#### Acceptance Criteria

- **Given** new issues created in `EPIC-PA-05` grouping (each issue's first-seen timestamp marking the first crash occurrence) and alerts generated in `EPIC-PA-08` (each alert's fired-at timestamp marking the first paging signal), **When** the reliability engineer queries the pipeline health dashboard for MTTD, **Then** MTTD is computed as the median time elapsed from each issue's first-seen timestamp to the first alert firing for that issue, over a documented look-back window (for example, 30 days), and the value is displayed alongside the trend.
- **Given** an MTTD trend computed over a rolling window (for example, 30 days with a 7-day rolling step), **When** the trend worsens (for example, median MTTD increases by more than a documented threshold over consecutive review windows), **Then** the team opens a reliability investigation that examines whether the alert thresholds in `EPIC-PA-08` need tuning (for example, the velocity-alert threshold is too lax for the new issue volume, or the regression-alert sensitivity has drifted), and the investigation outcome is recorded in the review minutes.
- **Given** an MTTD that is significantly faster than the median time-to-first-user-complaint for the same issues (measured by joining issue first-seen timestamps against an external complaint or support-ticket signal, where available), **When** the team reviews, **Then** the alert system is validated as effective at catching issues before users feel the pain and the validation is recorded as supporting evidence that the alerting investment in `EPIC-PA-08` is justified.
- **Given** an issue that escaped alerting in `EPIC-PA-08` (no alert fired despite the issue exceeding the documented impact threshold, for example a high-volume crash that did not breach the velocity threshold because volume ramped slowly), **When** the reliability engineer reviews the escape, **Then** the escape is recorded in the MTTD review, the alert threshold that should have fired is identified, and a tuning ticket is opened against `EPIC-PA-08` to close the detection gap.

#### Notes

- MTTD measurement closes the loop on the alerting system in `EPIC-PA-08` — without MTTD tracking, alert thresholds (velocity, regression, signal-to-noise) are tuned by intuition and by complaint volume rather than by observed outcomes; the team cannot tell whether the alerting configuration is too sensitive (excessive false positives, alert fatigue) or not sensitive enough (issues escape detection until users complain). Per industry-standard practice, the goal is to detect anomalies early (such as memory leaks or ANRs) before users feel the pain, and MTTD is the metric that quantifies how well the system is achieving that goal.
- The MTTD computation depends on accurate first-seen timestamps from `EPIC-PA-05` grouping (the moment when the first crash of a new fingerprint arrives at the server) and accurate fired-at timestamps from `EPIC-PA-08` alerts. Both are recorded in the `EPIC-PA-06` data substrate; this story does not introduce new instrumentation but does require that the BigQuery export includes both timestamp columns and that the join is documented in the dashboard's underlying query.
- MTTD trends are particularly informative around new-app-version rollouts (when previously-unseen issues first arrive at scale) and around third-party SDK or Android/iOS OS-version transitions (when known-good code paths begin exhibiting new failure modes); the dashboard should annotate these rollout events so the trend reader can correlate MTTD changes with their drivers rather than reading the trend in isolation.

### STORY-PA-10-S04: Codify error-budget policy and budget-burn responses

**As a** member of engineering leadership, **I want** a documented error-budget policy with named budget-burn responses, **so that** SLO breaches translate into principled, fair engineering tradeoffs between reliability and feature work rather than into ad-hoc reactions driven by whoever shouts loudest in the moment.

#### Acceptance Criteria

- **Given** the capture-rate SLO from `STORY-PA-10-S01` and the time-to-dashboard SLO from `STORY-PA-10-S02`, **When** the engineering leader documents the error-budget policy, **Then** the policy explicitly specifies a budget window (for example, 30 days rolling), a budget amount per SLO (for example, the team accepts a 1% breach allowance over each window, equivalent to roughly 7 hours of allowable time-to-dashboard SLO breach in a 30-day window), and a budget-burn response table that maps named burn thresholds to named team responses.
- **Given** the error-budget for an SLO is consumed beyond a documented burn-rate threshold (for example, 50% of the monthly budget is burned in the first 7 days of a 30-day window, indicating that the team will exhaust the budget well before the window resets if the burn rate continues), **When** the team reviews the burn signal in the pipeline health dashboard, **Then** the policy triggers a documented response — for example, halting non-reliability feature work in the affected component until the burn rate is brought back to baseline, or commencing a documented incident response — and the response is invoked by the named role authorized to invoke it.
- **Given** the error-budget for both SLOs is healthy (for example, less than 20% of the budget is burned through the first 50% of the window, indicating the team is operating well within the reliability bar), **When** the team reviews the budget signal, **Then** the policy explicitly permits aggressive feature investment without reliability guilt, and the engineering leader is empowered to greenlight feature work that may consume some budget without crossing into the breach band.
- **Given** the error-budget policy is documented in an accessible location (committed to the migration program documentation or a comparable durable surface), **When** new team members onboard or new stakeholders are introduced to the pipeline, **Then** they encounter the policy as part of their onboarding material, understand the named burn thresholds and named responses, and can independently interpret a budget-burn dashboard reading without needing tribal knowledge from existing team members.

#### Notes

- Error-budget policy is the standard SRE pattern for converting ambient quality concerns into measurable engineering tradeoffs; the canonical reference is the SRE workbook's chapter on error-budget policy and SLO consequence management. This story does not prescribe a specific SRE flavor (Google SRE workbook, Adaptive Capacity Labs, or similar) but does require that ONE coherent policy is documented and adopted, with the named burn thresholds and named responses spelled out concretely enough that an on-call engineer can apply the policy without management consultation in the moment.
- The specific budget percentages (1% breach allowance, 50% burn threshold) are team-specific and depend on the business's tolerance for reliability degradation versus feature velocity. The story does NOT prescribe specific percentages but DOES require that the policy is documented with concrete numbers (not "small" or "significant" qualitative descriptors) so the policy is operationally testable rather than rhetorical. Teams new to error-budget policy may start with conservative numbers (1% allowance, 50% burn trigger) and tune them after observing one or two real budget-burn events.
- The error-budget policy must be ratified by both engineering leadership AND product leadership for it to be effective — a policy that engineering adopts unilaterally and that product does not honor when the halt-feature-work response is invoked is a policy in name only. The story's last acceptance criterion requires the policy to be accessible to onboarding stakeholders precisely so the ratification context is preserved as the team turns over.

## Definition of Done

The epic is considered complete when ALL of the following observable conditions are simultaneously true:

- All 4 embedded stories (`STORY-PA-10-S01`, `STORY-PA-10-S02`, `STORY-PA-10-S03`, `STORY-PA-10-S04`) have their acceptance criteria satisfied.
- All 5 epic-level acceptance criteria above are satisfied.
- The pipeline health dashboard is wired against the `EPIC-PA-06` BigQuery export (or a comparable durable data surface) and displays, at a minimum: the capture-rate SLO and current 7-day rolling rate, the time-to-dashboard SLO and current synthetic-monitoring p50/p95/p99 latency, MTTD trend over a 30-day rolling window with rollout-event annotations, and current error-budget burn for each SLO.
- Synthetic monitoring runs at a documented cadence (for example, every 30 minutes against staging builds, with one run per supported client platform — Android, iOS, Flutter, Unity, React Native) and feeds the pipeline health dashboard's time-to-dashboard latency time-series with no documented gaps over the look-back window.
- The error-budget policy is documented in an accessible location (committed to the migration program documentation or a comparable durable surface), is ratified by both engineering and product leadership, and is referenced from new-team-member onboarding material so the policy survives team turnover.
- At least one historical SLO breach is exercised end-to-end (via a deliberate synthetic monitoring run designed to exceed the time-to-dashboard SLO, or via a deliberate temporary capture-rate-SLO breach in staging) and is observed to trigger the documented response — confirming that the SLO instrumentation, the alerting pathway, and the budget-burn response logic are all wired correctly and the policy is operationally testable rather than rhetorical.
- The post-migration capture rate is compared to the pre-migration Fabric baseline for the same device population and same time-of-day window per the `EPIC-MIG-09` test-crash validation outputs, the canonical +30% Android capture improvement is recorded as a one-time baseline reset in the SLO documentation, and the new baseline is adopted as the steady-state target for the capture-rate SLO.
- The Crashlytics debug logging procedure (`adb shell setprop log.tag.FirebaseCrashlytics DEBUG` on Android, `-FIRDebugEnabled` launch argument on iOS) is documented in the pipeline operations runbook as the canonical diagnostic step for time-to-dashboard SLO breaches and is linked from synthetic-monitoring alert payloads.
